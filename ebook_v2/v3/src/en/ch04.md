# Chapter 4 — The Body: Hands, Feet, Posture and Distance

Berlin, 1904. A horse called Hans does arithmetic. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, a maths teacher, asks him "what's three plus two?" — and Hans taps his hoof five times. He tells the time. He spells words. Crowds gather to watch. A commission of 13 experts examines the case and concludes there's no trick.

Three years later, a young psychologist, Oskar Pfungst, runs a simple experiment. When Hans could **see** the person asking, and that person knew the answer, he was right 89% of the time. When he **couldn't** see the questioner, he was right 6% of the time (Pfungst, 1907).

Hans couldn't do maths. He could read bodies. As he got close to the right number, the questioner tensed up — and on the final tap, relaxed without realising. Hans stopped there.

If a horse can read the human body, so can you.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

What Pfungst discovered is the core idea of this chapter: **the body talks even when the person doesn't want it to** — and Hans's questioners didn't even know they were giving cues. Pfungst repeated the experiment with people, playing the "horse" himself, and saw the same thing: at the right number, people lifted their heads slightly without noticing.

And we read these signals fast. In a famous study, Nalini Ambady and Robert Rosenthal showed strangers **silent** videos of teachers lecturing — just 30 seconds of each. The strangers' ratings came out very close to the ones students gave at the end of the semester, after months of classes (Ambady & Rosenthal, 1993). Even with 6 seconds, the result held.

In other words: you already read bodies — without a method. This chapter gives you the method.

### THE DISTANCE RULE

Former FBI agent Joe Navarro, who spent 25 years in interrogation and counterintelligence, popularised a practical rule: **the further a body part is from the face, the less controlled it tends to be** (Navarro, 2008). People train their smile. Almost nobody trains their feet.

### THE FEET

- **Feet pointing at the exit** — in a conversation, when the feet turn to the door, the person wants to leave, even if they're smiling.
- **Feet pointing at you** — interest and engagement.
- **Feet that suddenly freeze** — someone who was bouncing a foot and goes still may be reacting to something that was said.
- **Feet tucked under the chair** — withdrawal, discomfort.

### THE HANDS

- **Visible, open hands** — tend to go with comfort.
- **Hands that disappear** — into pockets, under the table — *at a specific moment* in the conversation.
- **Self-soothing gestures** — rubbing the neck, touching hair, rubbing the thighs. They don't mean lying; they mean stress.
- **Gestures that arrive late** — a genuine gesture goes with the word. A rehearsed one comes after it.

Notice that none of these works on its own. Always go back to Chapter 2: they only matter as **deviations from baseline**, tied to a topic.

### POSTURE AND MIRRORING

- **Leaning in** — interest, or a wish to dominate the space.
- **Leaning back** — distance, evaluation or disinterest.
- **Body turned sideways** — "I'm here, but not completely."
- **Taking up space** — open arms, things spread on the table. Linked to comfort and status.

When two people are in tune, they copy each other without noticing. Tanya Chartrand and John Bargh called it the **chameleon effect**: in one study, when someone discreetly mirrored a participant's posture and gestures, the participant liked them more and found the conversation smoother — without realising they were being mirrored (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999).

That proves two things at once. Natural mirroring is a good sign of connection. And anyone who knows the effect can **use it on purpose** to create a false sense of rapport. The difference: natural mirroring has a delay and imperfections; calculated mirroring is too fast and too complete.

### DISTANCE AND BOUNDARIES

Everyone has a "bubble" of space. Notice who enters it without asking — a touch on the arm too early, a hand on your back, standing too close.

Entering someone's space and seeing whether they react is a way of **testing boundaries**. And there's a pattern worth gold: people who test small boundaries early tend to test big ones later. You'll come back to this in Chapter 22.

### MYTH: "93% OF COMMUNICATION IS NON-VERBAL"

You'll hear this a lot. It comes from Albert Mehrabian's experiments in the 1960s, but those studies were about a very specific case — people saying a single word in a tone that contradicted it. Mehrabian himself warned that the numbers don't apply to communication in general. The body doesn't replace words. **It completes them.**

> The feet say where someone wants to go. The hands say what they're feeling. The face says what they want you to see.

The body gives you the feeling. But people also give themselves away in how they speak — and that's even less controlled than you'd think. That's Chapter 5.

Team lunch at a small tavern behind the office. Sofia sits next to Inês and keeps touching her arm as she talks — "you're really special, you know that?". Inês shifts her chair a little to reach the bread. A bit later, Sofia leans in again.

At dinner, Daniel is nervous, as always. When Inês says she's tired and would rather call it an early night, he says "of course", asks for the bill and walks her to the metro.

At her building, Rui is in the entrance chatting with the doorman and smiles at her. Sofia, who'd walked her home, appears at the door to give her back her jacket. Rui suddenly remembers he's left something in his car and goes out.
