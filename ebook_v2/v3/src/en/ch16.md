# Chapter 16 — The Six Triggers of Influence

New York, December 2008. Bernie Madoff, former chairman of the NASDAQ stock exchange, confesses to his sons that his investment fund is "one big lie". The next day he's arrested.

For decades, Madoff had promised what no one else did: steady returns, year after year, whether markets were up or down. And he didn't take just anyone. You had to be introduced. Some people waited months to get in. The most respected names were already there.

Reported losses reached around **$65 billion**. It was the largest Ponzi scheme in history. Madoff was sentenced to 150 years in prison.

The victims weren't fools: there were banks, foundations, professional investors. Madoff didn't fool them with a lie. He fooled them with **mental shortcuts** — several at once.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

In 1984, psychologist Robert Cialdini published *Influence*, after years of studying — and working undercover in sales, fundraising and advertising — how people are led to say "yes". He concluded there are **six shortcuts** we use to decide quickly (Cialdini, 1984). They're useful: without them, we couldn't function. But whoever knows them can use them against you.

And they work even on intelligent people — especially on them, because they trust their own judgement. No one thinks they can be manipulated. That's exactly what makes you manipulable.

### 1. RECIPROCITY

When someone gives us something, we feel obliged to give back.

- **Manipulative use:** an unrequested favour so they can later ask for something much bigger. A "gift" that leaves you in debt.
- **Defence:** thank the favour as a favour. "Thank you" doesn't mean "I owe you".

### 2. COMMITMENT AND CONSISTENCY

Once we've said "yes" to something small, we tend to say "yes" to the next thing, to look consistent.

Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser proved it in 1966. They asked homeowners to put a huge, ugly sign in their front garden saying "Drive Carefully". Only **17%** agreed. But in another group, weeks earlier, they'd asked for something tiny — a small sticker about safe driving. In that group, **76%** later agreed to the huge sign (Freedman & Fraser, 1966).

- **Manipulative use:** something small, then something bigger, then something huge. "Since you helped with this, could you…"
- **Defence:** judge each request as if it were the first. You have the right to change your mind.

### 3. SOCIAL PROOF

We follow what others do, especially when uncertain. It's Asch from Chapter 7, outside the lab.

- **Manipulative use:** "everyone's already agreed", "you're the only one who hasn't…", fake reviews, queues created on purpose. In Madoff's case: "the best people are already in".
- **Defence:** ask yourself "if nobody else were doing this, would I?".

### 4. AUTHORITY

We obey people who seem to have status. In 1963, Stanley Milgram asked 40 men to give stronger and stronger electric shocks to another person, just because a man in a grey lab coat told them to continue. The shocks were fake — but they didn't know. **65%** went all the way to the maximum (Milgram, 1963).

- **Manipulative use:** fake experts, invented titles, a tone of absolute certainty. Madoff had chaired the NASDAQ. Holmes had former cabinet secretaries on her board (Chapter 1).
- **Defence:** check the authority. Confidence in the voice isn't competence.

### 5. LIKING

We say "yes" to people we like — and we like people who resemble us, flatter us and mirror us (the chameleon effect from Chapter 4).

- **Manipulative use:** strategic compliments, "we have so much in common", calculated mirroring.
- **Defence:** separate the person from the proposal. "I like him" and "the proposal is good" are different decisions.

### 6. SCARCITY

We want more of what seems rare. In a classic study, the same cookies were rated more desirable when they were in a jar with **two** than in a jar with **ten** (Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975).

- **Manipulative use:** "today only", "last few left", "you have to decide now". In Madoff's case: "not everyone gets in".
- **Defence:** artificial urgency is a warning sign. Good opportunities rarely vanish in ten minutes.

> People with a good offer let you think. People with a bad offer need you not to.

Notice how many triggers Madoff used at once: authority, scarcity, social proof, consistency. Almost every scam uses three or more. And the place these triggers hide best behind "professionalism" is work. That's Chapter 17.

[protege]
Before any "yes" involving money or commitment, ask yourself: "Which trigger am I feeling right now?" Naming it takes away half its power.
[ajuda]
When an older relative tells you about a "once-in-a-lifetime opportunity" or an urgent call from "the bank", help them name the triggers: urgency, authority, scarcity. That's how almost every scam works.
:::

:::treino
In the next ad you see, identify which triggers it uses. Most use two or three at once. Once you see them, they stop working as well on you.

EPISODE 16 · THE WEEKEND
On a Thursday afternoon, Sofia asks Inês a favour: to cover Sofia's client over the weekend, because "my mum isn't well". Inês had arranged to go to Porto to see Carolina.

"I know it's a pain," says Sofia. "But after everything I did for you when you started… And you need to tell me today, Marta wants to know by six."

Inês covers the client. She cancels Porto.

On Monday, on Sofia's Instagram, there are photos from Saturday at a rooftop bar in Santos.

*How many triggers fit into three sentences?*
