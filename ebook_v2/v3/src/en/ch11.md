# Chapter 11 — Isolation: How They Pull You Away from the People Who Protect You

England, 14 August 2010. Sally Challen, 56, kills her husband, Richard, at home, with a hammer. She's convicted of murder the following year and sentenced to life.

The case looks simple. It wasn't.

Over decades of marriage, according to her sons and her lawyers, Richard controlled her: the money, the outings, the friendships. He humiliated her, cheated on her and stopped her going out with other people without him. Her friends gradually disappeared. In the end, she had almost no one.

In 2019, the Court of Appeal quashed the murder conviction. Sally Challen ended up convicted of manslaughter and walked free. The case became the first major court test of a new idea: **coercive control**.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Sociologist Evan Stark spent years studying cases like this and reached a conclusion that changed how abuse is seen: the most dangerous thing often isn't physical violence. It's **a pattern of control** that strips the victim, little by little, of freedom, resources and — above all — people (Stark, 2007). In England and Wales, coercive control has been a crime since 2015.

And isolation is the piece that makes all the others possible. Remember Asch, in Chapter 7: with one ally in the room, almost everyone resisted group pressure. **Isolation is the removal of that ally.** On your own, no one tells you "this isn't normal" — and without that outside voice, you accept more and more.

In other words: when someone pulls you away from your people, they're not just taking your time. They're switching off your alarm.

### IT'S NEVER DIRECT

No one says "drop your friends". It's done gradually, with apparent affection.

:::fig 7:::

The lines of isolation:

- **"Your friend is jealous of us."**
- **"Your family never valued you the way I do."**
- **"I prefer it when it's just the two of us."**
- **"They don't know you like I do."**
- **"I've noticed you're different after you see them."**

Each line, on its own, seems harmless. Together, they build a wall — the gap (Chapter 1) that only shows in the pattern.

### THE TACTICS

- **Creating conflict** — arguments every time you're about to see someone, so going out costs you.
- **Becoming the only option** — plans booked for the same days you'd already booked.
- **Poisoning the picture** — negative things about your friends, true or not.
- **Playing the victim** — "you're off with them and I'm stuck here alone".
- **Controlling resources** — money, car, phone, schedules. It's what Sally Challen's sons described.
- **Moving away** — "let's start over somewhere else".

### HOW YOU KNOW IT'S HAPPENING

- **You see your friends less and less** and aren't sure why.
- **You've started hiding** things about the relationship from people who love you.
- **You feel you have to "defend"** the person all the time.
- **Your friends have stopped inviting you** because you always say no.
- **You feel relief when the person isn't there** — and guilt for feeling relief.

> People who love you want you to have a full life. People who want to control you want to be your whole life.

When there's no one outside left to confirm what you see, the last step is making you doubt what you see. That's Chapter 12.

:::usa
[protege]
Set a fixed date with your people — a dinner a month, a call a week — and don't negotiate it with anyone. It's your Asch ally, with a date in the diary.
[ajuda]
If a friend has drifted away, don't stop inviting them just because they always say no. Keep going, without guilt or pressure: "That's fine. The invitation stands." For someone being isolated, knowing the door is still open is everything.
:::

:::treino
Write the names of the five people who mattered most to you two years ago. How many have you seen in the past month? If the list has shrunk and you don't know why, it's worth thinking about what changed.
