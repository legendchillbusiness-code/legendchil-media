# Chapter 12 — Gaslighting: When They Make You Doubt Your Own Memory

London, 1938. A play by Patrick Hamilton called *Gas Light* opens. Every night, a husband dims the gas lights in the house. When his wife notices the light fading, he assures her she's imagining things. He does the same with objects that go missing, with sounds in the attic, with everything she sees.

Little by little, she stops trusting her own mind.

In 1944, the film with Ingrid Bergman made the story famous. In 2022, *gaslighting* was Merriam-Webster's word of the year. Eighty years on, everyone knows the word. Almost no one recognises the thing while it's happening to them.

### WHAT IT IS

Gaslighting is when someone **repeatedly denies your reality** until you start doubting your memory, your perception and your judgement.

"I never said that." "You're exaggerating." "That never happened." "You're too sensitive."

### WHY THIS MATTERS

For years, gaslighting was talked about as if it were just a psychological problem between two people. Sociologist Paige Sweet showed it's more than that. She interviewed 43 women who had survived domestic violence and found that gaslighting works because **it leans on ideas society already holds** — such as that certain people are "too emotional" or "unstable" — and on institutions where the victim already has little credibility (Sweet, 2019).

In other words: whoever gaslights you doesn't need to convince you from scratch. They use doubts that already exist — and, after the isolation of Chapter 11, there's no one outside left to contradict them.

### HOW IT WORKS

1. **Denial** — they deny what they said or did, even with proof.
2. **Minimising** — "it was just a joke", "it was nothing".
3. **Reversal** — the problem becomes your reaction, not what they did.
4. **Repetition** — so many times you stop trusting yourself.
5. **Dependence** — since you no longer trust your judgement, you start trusting theirs.

Notice step 3: it's the "attack the questioner" from Chapter 5, turned into a system.

### THE SIGNS IN YOU

Gaslighting is spotted more by what it makes you feel than by what the person does:

- **You apologise constantly**, without quite knowing why.
- **You doubt your memory** in situations that used to be clear.
- **You feel like you're "going crazy".**
- **You've started saving messages** to prove to yourself what happened.
- **You make excuses** for the person's behaviour to others.
- **You no longer know what's you exaggerating** and what's real.

> If you need proof to believe your own memory, the problem isn't your memory.

### HOW TO PROTECT YOURSELF

- **Write it down.** A simple, private record of what happens, with dates. Remember Chapter 6: what's written protects you. It's not for arguing — it's for you.
- **Don't argue about reality.** With someone who gaslights, debating "what happened" never ends. Say "I remember it differently" and close it.
- **Check outside.** Talk to someone you trust. It's Asch's ally again: an outside view gives you back your footing.
- **Look at the pattern, not the episode.** Anyone can misremember once. Denying your reality as a habit is something else.

Gaslighting makes you doubt yourself. The next tactic makes you doubt your place — by bringing a third person into the relationship. That's Chapter 13.

EPISODE 12 · THE SLACK MESSAGE
At Tuesday's meeting, Marta asks why the client got the old version of the presentation. Inês remembers perfectly: on Friday, Sofia messaged her on Slack, "send v2, v3 isn't approved yet".

After the meeting, she tells her so. Sofia frowns. "Me? I never said that. Inês, you've been so tired… It's not the first time you've got mixed up, is it? I just want to help you."

That night, Inês opens Slack to check. The message is there. She stares at it for a long time, not quite knowing what to do with it.
