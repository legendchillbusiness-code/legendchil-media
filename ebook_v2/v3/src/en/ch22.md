# Chapter 22 — Boundaries That Aren't Up for Negotiation

Tokyo, 27 July 2021. The women's gymnastics team final at the Olympic Games. Simone Biles, 24, Olympic champion, the most famous gymnast in the world, does one vault — and leaves the arena with the team doctor.

When she comes back, she doesn't compete again. "I have to focus on my mental health," she tells reporters. "There's more to life than just gymnastics."

Millions watching, a team depending on her, a country waiting for gold. And she said no.

A week later, she came back for one event and won bronze on the beam. But what stayed from those Games wasn't the medal. It was the boundary.

### WHAT A BOUNDARY IS

A boundary isn't a request. It's a decision about **what you do** when someone crosses a line.

*"Don't shout at me"* is a request. *"If you shout, I'll leave the conversation"* is a boundary.

Biles didn't ask anyone to stop pressuring her. She decided what she herself was going to do.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

A boundary you don't keep teaches the other person exactly how far they can go. Remember Chapter 4: people who test small boundaries early tend to test big ones later. And without boundaries, everything you've learned so far isn't worth much: **you see everything — and accept everything**.

There's another reason. Anthropologist Robin Dunbar showed that our relationships are organised in **layers**: a very small core of very close people (around 5), then about 15, then about 50, up to roughly 150 stable relationships (Dunbar, 2010). There isn't room for everyone at the centre.

In other words: **trust is limited by nature**. Deciding who goes in each layer isn't coldness. It's managing a resource that isn't enough for everyone.

### YOUR CORE BOUNDARIES

Define them before you need them:

- **Respect** — I don't accept insults, shouting or humiliation, not even as a joke.
- **Privacy** — my phone, my accounts and my conversations are mine.
- **Time** — I'm not available 24 hours a day. I reply when I can (Chapter 6).
- **Money** — I don't lend or invest under pressure (Chapter 16).
- **Relationships** — no one decides who I talk to (Chapter 11).
- **Body** — my "no" is final.

### HOW TO COMMUNICATE A BOUNDARY

1. **Short** — one sentence.
2. **Calm** — no anger, no apologising.
3. **With a consequence** — what *you* will do, not what the other person has to do.
4. **Kept** — if you don't keep it, you've taught them your boundary is negotiable.

Example: *"When you raise your voice, I end the conversation. We'll pick it up when we're both calm."* And if the voice rises, you end it.

### HOW PEOPLE REACT

- **Healthy people** — may be surprised, but adjust.
- **Manipulative people** — test the boundary. Once, twice, three times. They take offence. They play the victim (the Victim Mask, Chapter 1). They say you've changed.

> Someone's reaction to your boundary tells you everything you need to know about them.

### THE CIRCLES OF TRUST

:::fig 10:::

- **Inner circle** — 2 to 5 people who have proved their loyalty in hard times. They know almost everything.
- **Middle circle** — trusted friends and colleagues. They know enough.
- **Outer circle** — acquaintances. They know the basics.

No one jumps from outside to inside in a few weeks. Access is earned with **time and proof**, not intensity — and jumping circles fast is exactly what the Loyalty Mask (Chapter 1) and love bombing (Chapter 10) do.

A boundary protects you inside a relationship. Sometimes it isn't enough — and the only boundary left is leaving. That's Chapter 23.
