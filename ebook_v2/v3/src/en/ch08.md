# Chapter 8 — Why They Choose You

New York, 1981. Two researchers, Betty Grayson and Morris Stein, film 60 people walking down a Manhattan street in the middle of the morning. Ordinary people, on their way to work or the shops.

Then they show the videos to men convicted of violent crimes and ask a simple question: **who would you pick?**

The choices repeat. And they're not the smallest or most fragile people. They're the ones who **walk a certain way**: uneven steps, head down, eyes on the ground, movements that look disconnected from the body (Grayson & Stein, 1981). The prisoners couldn't explain why. They just knew.

Predators read people too. This chapter is about what they read in you.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Part I taught you to read others. But the most important read is the one you do on yourself — because the tactics you'll meet in the next chapters are built to fit your open doors.

And the people looking for those doors are good at it. In a study of 47 inmates at a maximum-security prison in Canada, those who scored highest on the **interpersonal and affective traits of psychopathy** — superficial charm, low empathy — were **more accurate** at guessing, from gait alone, who had been a victim before. And they were the ones most likely to say they'd looked at the walk (Wheeler, Book & Costello, 2009).

In other words: reading yourself isn't a self-help exercise. It's defence. If you don't know where you're vulnerable, someone will find out before you do.

### WHAT THEY LOOK FOR

They don't look for weakness. They look for **usefulness and access**:

- **High empathy** — you feel other people's pain as your own. The manipulator turns it into obligation (remember the Victim Mask in Chapter 1).
- **Moments of transition** — the end of a relationship, a move to a new city, grief, losing a job. You're more open, with less of a net around you.
- **Loneliness** — people with few people around them have few people to warn them. Remember Asch (Chapter 7): one ally is enough to break the pressure. With no allies, no one breaks it.
- **Need for approval** — trouble saying "no", fear of letting people down.
- **Too much responsibility** — you feel you have to fix everyone's problems.
- **Optimism about people** — "everyone has a good side".

Notice something: almost all of these are **qualities**. That's exactly why they get exploited.

> They don't choose you because you're weak. They choose you because you're useful — and available.

### IT'S NOT YOUR FAULT

Responsibility for manipulation always belongs to the manipulator. Full stop.

The 60 pedestrians in New York did nothing wrong. They were just walking. Knowing your open doors isn't accepting blame — it's gaining the ability to close them when you need to.

### HOW TO READ YOURSELF

Answer honestly:

1. What stage of life am I in right now? Stable, or in transition?
2. If something goes wrong, who do I tell?
3. Who do I find it hardest to say "no" to?
4. Which compliment disarms me fastest?
5. When did I last feel I had to "earn" someone's affection?

The answers show you where someone would get in. In the next seven chapters you'll see exactly *how* — starting with the kind of person who does this on purpose.

:::usa
[protege]
In times of transition — a break-up, a move, a loss — slow everything down. No big decisions, no new relationships at high speed. That's when you're easiest to read.
[ajuda]
If someone close to you has just been through a break-up, a move or a loss, stay closer during that time. It's when they most need a net — and when predators show up most.
:::

> Close your doors before someone finds them.
