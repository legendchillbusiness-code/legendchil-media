# Chapter 21 — Lines to Answer With: Scripts for Every Tactic

New York University and the University of Sheffield, 2006. Psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pull together 94 studies on a simple question: why do so many people know what they should do — and then, in the moment, don't do it?

The answer they found is almost embarrassingly simple. People who decided **in advance**, in the format *"if X happens, then I'll do Y"*, followed through far more often than people who only had the intention. The effect was medium to large — rare in psychology (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

They called them **implementation intentions**. This chapter calls them something else: **scripts**.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

When someone manipulates you, the goal is to knock you off balance. Under pressure, nobody thinks well — remember Vrij, in Chapter 1: with a busy mind, control fails. That applies to you too. Manipulators count on you hunting for the perfect answer and, while you hunt, giving in.

In other words: **the best answer isn't the cleverest one. It's the one you already had ready.** Notice the format of the study — *if… then…* — it's the same pattern as Mischel and Shoda (Chapter 1), now working for you. Instead of reading other people's patterns, you create your own.

Say the lines calmly, without raising your voice, and don't justify them.

### AGAINST RUSHING (SCARCITY, URGENCY — CHAPTER 16)

- "I don't decide anything under pressure. I'll let you know tomorrow."
- "If it has to be now, then it's no."

### AGAINST GUILT (CHAPTER 18)

- "I appreciate what you did. It doesn't change my answer."
- "I can care about you and still say no."

### AGAINST GASLIGHTING (CHAPTER 12)

- "I remember it differently. We're not going to agree on this."
- "I don't need you to agree with my memory."

### AGAINST DISGUISED CRITICISM

- "What do you mean?" — and wait. It forces the person to say directly what they implied.
- "Was that meant to be funny?"

### AGAINST TRIANGULATION (CHAPTER 13)

- "I'm not going to compete with anyone. If you prefer someone else, say so."

### AGAINST LOVE BOMBING (CHAPTER 10)

- "I'm enjoying getting to know you. I'd rather take it slowly."
- "It's early for that. Let's see how it goes."

### AGAINST ISOLATION (CHAPTER 11)

- "My friends are important to me. That's not up for discussion."

### AGAINST THE SILENT TREATMENT (CHAPTER 14)

- "Whenever you want to talk, I'm here." — and get on with your life.

### AGAINST "YOU'RE OVERREACTING"

- "Maybe. Even so, that's how I feel, and I want to talk about it."

### AGAINST THE TRAP FAVOUR AND THE INVASIVE QUESTION

- "Thanks, but I'd rather not."
- "I'd rather not talk about that." — no explanation.

> A "no" doesn't need defending. The more you justify yourself, the more doors you open for negotiation.

### THE BROKEN RECORD

In the 1970s, psychologist Manuel J. Smith described a technique that's still one of the most effective against persistence: **repeating the same sentence, in the same tone, without adding arguments** (Smith, 1975).

"I understand. My answer is no." Three times, if needed.

It works because manipulation needs **material**: every justification you give is a new door to negotiate through. The broken record gives them nothing to hold on to.

Lines protect you in the moment. But there's something stronger than a good line: a line you decided on before anyone tried to cross it. That's Chapter 22.

Inês breathes. "I won't be able to. I've got plans."

"Seriously, Inês? Like that?"

"I understand. I won't be able to."

Sofia turns her back without another word. Inês's hands shake under the desk for ten minutes. Then she texts Carolina: "Is Saturday still on?"
