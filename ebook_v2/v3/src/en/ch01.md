# Chapter 1 — The Three Masks and the Gap

In 2013, Tyler Shultz was 22 and had just joined the most talked-about company in the world: Theranos. Its founder, Elizabeth Holmes, promised hundreds of lab tests from a single drop of blood.

Investors had put almost a billion dollars into it. Its board included former US cabinet secretaries — among them Tyler's grandfather.

Everyone believed. But Tyler worked in the lab. And in the lab he saw something no one else saw: **the results didn't add up.**

This chapter is about what Tyler saw — and why so many brilliant people didn't see the same thing.

### EVERYONE WEARS A MASK

Let's start with the least obvious part: masks aren't a manipulator thing. They're a human thing.

The sociologist Erving Goffman compared social life to a theatre: there's a **stage**, where we perform, and there's **backstage**, where the performance stops (Goffman, 1959). It isn't hypocrisy. It's social survival.

And lying is part of the performance — more than we think. In a study where 147 people logged every lie they told in a diary, the average was **one to two lies a day**, almost all of them small (DePaulo et al., 1996). In other words: if everyone lies a little every day, everyone wears a mask. You're not looking for *who* has a mask — you're looking for *who uses it against you*.

So the useful question is never "does this person wear a mask?". It's: **"how far is the mask from the face?"**

### THE THREE LAYERS

- **Public Mask** — what they show strangers. Polished, rehearsed. The "CV" version.
- **Social Mask** — what they show friends and colleagues. Looser, still filtered.
- **Private Face** — real fears, desires and ambitions. It only appears with time, intimacy or pressure.

Holmes's **Public Mask** was flawless: the Steve Jobs black turtleneck, the deep voice, the unblinking stare, the story of the genius who dropped out of college at 19.

Her **Social Mask** was the one she showed the powerful men on her board: attentive, loyal, admiring.

Her **Private Face** was in the lab. And that's where Tyler was.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Think you would have noticed? Probably not.

When **206 studies** on lie detection were pooled, with more than 24,000 people judging, the accuracy rate was **54%** — barely better than flipping a coin (Bond & DePaulo, 2006). Worse: we catch truths far better than lies. The brain is built to believe.

That isn't a flaw — it's what lets us live in society. But anyone who knows how to build a good mask is counting on it.

What this means for you is simple: **your gut isn't enough.** You need a method — and that method is the gap, coming up next.

**Myth: "liars avoid eye contact."** When 158 supposed signs of lying were reviewed, most of them — gaze included — showed a weak link or none at all with lying (DePaulo et al., 2003). There's no single gesture that gives a liar away. There are patterns.

The cabinet secretaries on the Theranos board weren't naive. They were just judging **the wrong layer**.

### THE GAP IS THE SIGNAL

Don't try to guess someone's Private Face. Look for the **gap**: the distance between what a person says they are and what they repeatedly do.

Why "repeatedly"? Because people aren't consistent in general — they're consistent in their **"if… then…" patterns**: *if* they're contradicted, *then* they attack; *if* asked for details, *then* they change the subject (Mischel & Shoda, 1995). That's where the real person lives. It proves the core rule of this book: **a moment says nothing; a pattern says almost everything.**

Examples of gaps:

- **Says they value respect** — but interrupts everyone.
- **Says they're transparent** — but tenses up at simple questions.
- **Says they don't care about money** — but talks about prices in every conversation.
- **Says they're fiercely loyal** — but trashes every old friend.

At Theranos the gap was huge: the promise was "hundreds of tests on our machine"; the reality, later revealed by the Wall Street Journal, was that the machine ran only a small fraction of them. Tyler saw the gap because he looked at what repeated every day.

> A single gap is noise. A gap that repeats is information.

### THREE MASKS TO RECOGNISE

- **The Victim Mask** — everything happens to them, nothing is ever their fault. *It asks for your empathy.*
- **The Competence Mask** — huge promises, zero detail. *It asks for your trust.*
- **The Loyalty Mask** — "you're like a brother to me" after two weeks. *It asks for access.*

Holmes wore the **Competence** mask. The tell: **the more specific your question, the vaguer the answer.**

And **Loyalty** is the most dangerous, because it turns people against whoever doubts. When Tyler told his grandfather what he was seeing, he was rebuked. His grandfather sided with Holmes.

> People who work hard to convince you of who they are rarely are.

### THE MOMENT THE MASK SLIPS

Keeping a mask on is tiring. And when the mind is busy, control fails.

In a study with 55 police officers, asking suspects to tell their story **backwards** — a task that overloads the brain — raised lie detection from **46% to 58%** (Vrij et al., 2008). The lesson: you don't need to be a detective. You just need to notice what happens when someone's mind is busy.

In real life, three moments do the same thing:

- **Stress** — no energy left to perform.
- **Surprise** — no time to prepare the answer.
- **Frustration** — they lost, were contradicted, didn't get what they wanted.

At Theranos, that moment came when Tyler started asking questions. The company didn't answer with data. **It answered with lawyers.**

You don't need to provoke these moments. Just notice them when they happen. How someone treats the waiter who got their order wrong tells you more than ten pleasant dinners with you.

### WHAT THE GAP IS NOT

- **A mask isn't a lie.** Everyone has a stage and a backstage.
- **A gap can be insecurity.** The pattern tells you *where* to look, not *why*.
- **Don't diagnose.** The goal is to decide how much access someone has earned.

### HOW IT ENDED

In 2015, the Wall Street Journal exposed Theranos. Tyler was one of the sources. The company collapsed. In 2022, Holmes was convicted of fraud and later sentenced to more than 11 years in prison.

Tyler had no special gift. He did one thing: **he compared what he was told with what he saw, over and over** — and trusted the pattern, not the mask.

That's what you're going to learn to do.

But to see a gap, you first need to know what someone's *normal* is. Without that, you'll see lies where there are only nerves. That's where Chapter 2 begins.

EPISODE 1 · ARRIVAL
Inês is 27 and arrived in Lisbon on a Sunday night with a car full of boxes. New job at a communications agency, a one-bedroom flat in Arroios, no friends in the city. Her four-year relationship had ended in June.

On Monday, the first person to talk to her is Sofia, from the same team. She shows her the coffee machine, lends her a phone charger, tells her where you can eat well for under ten euros. "Everyone here is nice, but there's a lot of talking behind backs. I stay out of it." At lunch, between the dish of the day and dessert, she mentions that Marco, across the open-plan office, "had a serious problem with the boss — but that stays between us." Before they pay, she asks: "And you, did you come on your own? Boyfriend back in Porto?"

On Thursday, Inês has her first date with Daniel, whom she met on an app. He'd warned her: "I'm very punctual." He arrives twenty minutes late, apologising — the metro stopped at Alameda. He talks fast, laughs at himself, looks more at his glass than at her.

The neighbour next door is called Rui. Early forties, quiet. On Friday they pass on the stairs and he asks which agency she works at. She tells him. "Ah," he says. "I know it." And he goes up.

That night, sitting on the floor among the boxes, Inês thinks Lisbon might not be so hard after all. She already has a friend.

*Who would you trust?*
