# Chapter 6 — Reading Text Messages

United States, 2012. Manti Te'o is one of the stars of college football at the University of Notre Dame. During the season he tells reporters the story that moves the country: his girlfriend, Lennay Kekua, 22, died of leukaemia in September — on the same day as his grandmother.

In January 2013, the website Deadspin reveals that Lennay Kekua **never existed**. The photos belonged to another woman, who had no idea they were being used. The relationship had happened entirely through messages, social media and calls. The two had never met. The person who created the character, a former schoolmate of Te'o's, later admitted to inventing her.

Te'o wasn't naive. He'd just met someone through one channel only — the written one — which **hides almost everything you learned in the previous chapters**.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

By text there's no face, no feet, no tone. Only words — and words can be chosen, revised, deleted.

You'd expect us to lie more in writing. The research says something more interesting. When Jeffrey Hancock and colleagues asked 30 students to log every communication and every lie for a week, the share of conversations containing lies was **37% on the phone**, **27% face to face**, **21% on instant messaging** and **14% by email** (Hancock et al., 2004). The authors' explanation: we lie less where there's a **record**.

That gives you an advantage you don't have in conversation. In messages, words stay written — and what matters most isn't what they say, it's the **pattern**: when they arrive, how they change, what disappears.

### THE DIGITAL BASELINE

As with the body (Chapter 2), first learn the normal:

- **How long** do they usually take to reply?
- Do they write **long or short** messages?
- Do they use **emojis**? Which ones?
- **Who usually starts** conversations?
- **What time** do they talk to you?

### THE DEVIATIONS THAT MATTER

- **Reply times that change consistently** — from minutes to hours, for weeks. Something has changed.
- **Shrinking messages** — from paragraphs to "ok", "yeah", "k".
- **Usual emojis disappearing** — the emotional temperature drops.
- **Answering only the easy part** — you send three questions, they answer one.
- **New hours** — only talks by day, never at night; or the reverse.

### "SEEN" AND SILENCE

Leaving you on read once is nothing. Leaving you on read **as a pattern**, alternating with bursts of attention, is one of the most effective control tactics there is. It's called intermittent reinforcement — you'll see in Chapter 14 why it hooks so hard.

### SIGNS OF INTEREST AND WARNING SIGNS

Real interest by text:

- **Asks about you** — doesn't just reply.
- **Remembers** what you said days ago.
- **Starts conversations** without needing anything.
- **Replies in proportion** to what you send.

Warning signs:

- **Intensity too early** — 3 a.m. messages, "I've never felt this way" in week one.
- **Demanding instant replies** — "Why did you take 20 minutes?"
- **Switching channels when things get serious** — "let's talk later", "not something to put in writing".
- **Deleting messages**, or preferring disappearing messages for ordinary things. Remember Hancock's study: people who don't want a record have a reason.
- **Rewriting history** — "I never said that", when it's right there in writing.

In the Te'o case, one signal outweighed all the others: for months, **there was never a video call or a meeting**. There was always a reason — the hospital, an accident, the illness. When someone consistently avoids the one channel that would show their face, the gap (Chapter 1) lives in that silence.

> In messages, don't read the words. Read the pattern. Words can be chosen. The pattern gives them away.

So far you've read one person at a time. But you're almost never with just one person. In a group there's an invisible structure of power — and whoever sees it first has the advantage. That's Chapter 7.

:::usa
[protege]
Don't reply on the spot to pressure messages ("why did you take so long?"). Reply at your normal pace. Your pace is your baseline — don't let anyone rewrite it.
[ajuda]
If a friend shows you a chat and asks "am I overreacting?", don't argue about one line. Show them the pattern: who starts, who apologises, who demands.
:::

:::treino
Open the chat with someone important to you and scroll through the last few weeks. Don't read the content — just look at the rhythm: who starts, how long each of you takes, the length of the messages. The pattern tells you what the words don't.
:::

EPISODE 6 · 23:47
Thursday, 23:47. A message from Sofia:

"I don't want to be mean… but I think I saw Daniel on the app again 👀 Maybe it's nothing. I just thought you should know. Love you lots."

Two minutes later another one arrives, which Inês only sees in the morning: "This message was deleted."

Inês asks Daniel, half afraid. He replies twenty minutes later, as he usually does: "I deleted my account the day after I met you. Want to see?" And he sends a screenshot.

That week, Sofia messages her almost every night, late. About work, about life, about Daniel.

*What would you do with the 23:47 message?*
