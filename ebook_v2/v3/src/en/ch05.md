# Chapter 5 — Voice and Words

26 January 1998. The President of the United States, Bill Clinton, looks into the cameras at the White House and says slowly, jabbing his finger: *"I did not have sexual relations with that woman, Miss Lewinsky."*

Notice three words: **"that woman"**. Monica Lewinsky had worked near him for months. He knew her name — and said it straight after. But first, he spoke of her like a stranger.

Seven months later, Clinton admitted the relationship. The distance had been there from day one. Not in his face. In the **sentence**.

### PEOPLE CHOOSE THEIR WORDS. THEY RARELY CHOOSE HOW THEY SAY THEM.

A liar has to do two things at once: **invent the content** and **control the form**. Remember Vrij's study in Chapter 1: when the mind is busy, control fails. In language it's the same — which is why analysing words is often more reliable than analysing the body.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Psychologist James Pennebaker and colleagues asked 287 people to write and talk about topics like abortion — sometimes saying what they believed, sometimes arguing the opposite. Then a computer program analysed only the *form* of the words, ignoring the content (Newman et al., 2003).

The result: the program told liars from truth-tellers **61% to 67%** of the time. Human judges reading the same texts managed **52%** — basically chance. And the liars had a clear pattern:

- **Fewer "I"s** — they distance themselves from the story.
- **More negative words** — tension, discomfort.
- **Fewer words like "but" and "except"** — simpler stories, with less nuance.

In other words: **"that woman"** wasn't an accident. It was exactly the distancing research finds in lies. And here's the important bit: people miss it because they're listening to *what* is said, not *how*.

### THE VOICE

- **Pitch rising** — stress and tension tend to push it up.
- **Pace changing** — someone fast who slows on one topic, or the reverse.
- **Pauses before simple answers** — "Where were you last night?" shouldn't need three seconds.
- **Throat-clearing, coughing, swallowing** at specific moments.
- **Volume dropping** at the end of important sentences.

As always: these only count as **deviations from baseline** (Chapter 2).

### THE WORDS THAT GIVE PEOPLE AWAY

- **Over-explaining** — truth-tellers answer. Story-builders justify.
- **Honesty claims** — "honestly", "to be fair", "I swear", "why would I lie?". People telling the truth rarely feel the need to announce it.
- **Answering with a question** — "Why are you asking me that?" buys time.
- **Repeating the question** — "Was I with her last night?" also buys time.
- **Distancing** — "that woman"; "the car got scratched" instead of "I scratched the car". It's Pennebaker's "fewer I's".
- **Denial without a denial** — asked "Did you take the money?", answering "I'd never do something like that" instead of "No".
- **Lopsided detail** — lots of detail on what doesn't matter, little on the critical moment.
- **Attacking the questioner** — "You're always so suspicious of me!" turns the conversation from the question to you.

> A direct answer to a direct question is rare. When the question is simple and the answer is complicated, something is being managed.

### HOW TO ASK QUESTIONS THAT REVEAL

What works with language is **raising the load** without accusing:

- **Open question** — "Tell me how it went", instead of "Did you go?".
- **The story backwards** — from end to start. It's the method from Vrij's study (Chapter 1): invented stories are built in order and are hard to take apart.
- **An unexpected question** about a detail — "What music was playing?". People who lived it remember, or say "I didn't notice" naturally. People who invented it hesitate.
- **Silence after the answer** — many people fill silence with more than they meant to say.

These days, half of our conversations happen without a voice or a face. They happen in writing — and messages leave traces a conversation doesn't. That's Chapter 6.

When the answer to a simple question is complicated, repeat the question — slowly, word for word, without accusing. People telling the truth answer. People managing a story run out of road.

The open questions in this chapter aren't only for taking stories apart. With someone going through something hard, they're the gentlest way to help them tell a truth that hurts.

Next time someone tells you a story, count how often they use words like "honestly", "actually" or "I swear". Don't accuse. Just note it. You'll start hearing patterns you used to miss.

EPISODE 5 · THE REPORT
On Tuesday, Marta calls Inês into her office. The client report arrived late and the client complained. "Sofia told me she was waiting on your part." Inês had sent her part on Friday, at five in the afternoon.

That afternoon, she asks Sofia: "Did you tell Marta the delay was my fault?"

Sofia puts down her mug. "Why are you asking me that? Honestly, Inês… The report went out late, that's all, it happens to everyone. I'd never do something like that to a friend. Least of all you, after everything I've done to help you."

Inês apologises for asking.

That night, she asks Daniel if he still talks to his ex. "No. It ended because she cheated on me. It hurt, but it's over."

*Who answered the question?*
