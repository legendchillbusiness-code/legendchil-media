# Chapter 17 — Manipulation at Work

San Francisco, September 2016. Wells Fargo, one of the biggest banks in the United States, agrees to pay $185 million in fines. For years, employees had opened **more than two million** bank accounts and cards that customers had probably never asked for. Around **5,300** people had been fired over it.

But the interesting question isn't why 5,300 people did something wrong. It's why so many ordinary people did it at the same time.

The answer, according to regulators, lay in aggressive sales targets and the incentive system — daily pressure, impossible goals and an environment where people who missed targets were afraid. No one told anyone to lie. The system did it for them.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Work is where manipulation is most **acceptable** — because it hides behind "professionalism", "targets" and "that's how it works".

And remember Babiak's study, in Chapter 9: the managers with the most psychopathic traits were rated **more charismatic** and **worse at actual performance**. In other words, at work the Competence Mask (Chapter 1) doesn't just exist — it often gets **promoted**. People who can't read this end up doing the work and losing the credit.

### THE PROFILES

- **The credit thief** — in meetings says "we did it"; with the boss says "I did it".
- **The friendly saboteur** — smiles to your face, undermines you in conversations you're not in.
- **The permanent-urgency boss** — everything is for yesterday, so you never have time to think or negotiate. It's the scarcity trigger (Chapter 16) as a management style.
- **The favour colleague** — always helps you, then collects at the worst moments. Reciprocity (Chapter 16).
- **The information gatekeeper** — controls what you know, so you depend on them. It's the Shadow from Chapter 7.

### THE TACTICS

- **Meetings with no record** — verbal agreements that later "nobody remembers".
- **Vague requests** — if the task isn't clear, it's always your fault.
- **Public praise, private criticism** — or the reverse, whichever suits.
- **Professional triangulation** — "your colleague thinks that…" (Chapter 13).
- **Isolation** — leaving you off the emails, meetings or lunches where decisions are made (Chapter 11).

### HOW TO PROTECT YOURSELF

- **Put everything in writing.** After every important conversation, send a short email: "Just to confirm what we agreed: …". Remember Hancock (Chapter 6): when there's a record, people lie less.
- **Make your work visible.** Regular, direct updates to whoever makes the decisions.
- **Ask for clarity.** "To make sure I get this right: what does success look like for this task, and by when?"
- **Build relationships in several directions.** Don't depend on one person for information or reputation.
- **Read the room** (Chapter 7). Knowing who the Shadow is in your company is worth more than a lot of competence.

> At work, whoever controls the information controls the narrative. Make sure your version is in writing too.

At work, you can change jobs. There's one place where leaving is much harder — because the manipulation comes from people we love, and we call it "normal". That's Chapter 18.
