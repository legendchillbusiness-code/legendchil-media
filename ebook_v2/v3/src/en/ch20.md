# Chapter 20 — The Signs That Announce Betrayal

Virginia, February 2001. FBI agent Robert Hanssen is arrested in a park, after leaving secret documents hidden under a wooden footbridge. For about 22 years, on and off, he had sold secrets to the Soviet Union and then Russia. He received more than $1.4 million in cash and diamonds. He was sentenced to life in prison.

The most striking thing isn't what he did. It's **how many times the signs appeared** — and were explained away.

In 1990, his sister-in-law found a pile of cash in the house, and his brother-in-law, also FBI, recommended an investigation. Nothing happened. In 1993, Hanssen hacked into a colleague's computer and printed a classified document; he explained he was just demonstrating security flaws, and they accepted it. Later, he was caught installing a password-cracking program on his own computer; he got only a warning. In his final years, he repeatedly searched for his own name in FBI files.

Each sign, on its own, had an explanation. Together, they told a story no one read.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

Betrayal is rarely a bolt from a clear blue sky. There are almost always clouds first — it's just that, up close, we don't want to see them.

And the Hanssen case shows why: it isn't a lack of signs. It's that each sign gets judged **on its own**, and each one has a reasonable explanation. It's exactly the mistake this book has taught you to avoid since Chapter 1: **a moment says nothing; a pattern says almost everything.** And since Chapter 2: what counts is the **change from baseline**.

Betrayal, here, is any serious breach of trust: infidelity, a business partner who stabs you in the back, a friend who sells your secrets, a colleague who sabotages you.

### THE FIVE SIGNS

1. **Routine changes with no explanation** — new hours, new habits, new "meetings". Change happens; the sign is the **lack of a natural explanation**, or an explanation that keeps changing.
2. **Sudden protection of privacy** — the phone now lies face down, new passwords, leaving the room to take calls. Everyone has privacy; the sign is the **sudden change** (Chapter 2).
3. **Emotional distance and irritation** — less curiosity about you, more impatience, new criticisms. Sometimes the person betraying needs to see you as "the problem" to justify what they're doing.
4. **Overcompensation** — unexpected gifts, sudden attention, unusual niceness. Guilt sometimes shows up as generosity.
5. **Stories that don't fit** — details that change when the story is told again, long answers to simple questions (Chapter 5), aggressive defensiveness at neutral questions.

### AT WORK AND IN FRIENDSHIPS

- **A business partner who stops sharing numbers** or starts talking directly to your clients.
- **A friend who knows things** you only told one person.
- **A colleague who starts having meetings with your boss without you.**
- **Someone who stops telling you plans** they used to share.

> One sign is coincidence. Several signs at once, starting at the same time, are a story.

### WHAT TO DO

- **Don't accuse based on one sign.** You could be wrong — and an unfair accusation destroys trust too (the Othello error, Chapter 2).
- **Watch the pattern for a while.** Write down what you see, with dates.
- **Ask open, calm questions** — "I've noticed you've seemed different. Is everything OK?" The reaction tells you a lot.
- **Protect yourself in parallel.** Access to accounts, documents, sensitive information: review who has what.

You've finished Part III. You know how to see. In Part IV you'll learn to do something with what you see — starting with the exact lines to use when someone tries to manipulate you.
