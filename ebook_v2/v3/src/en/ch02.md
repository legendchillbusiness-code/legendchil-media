# Chapter 2 — The Baseline: Each Person's Normal

Perugia, Italy, November 2007. Meredith Kercher, a British student, is found dead in her room. Her housemate, the American Amanda Knox, is 20.

In the days that follow, police and newspapers fixate on her behaviour. According to police testimony, she did stretches and cartwheels at the station. She was photographed kissing her boyfriend outside the house where the crime happened. She didn't cry "the way she should have".

To a lot of people, that was guilt. Amanda Knox was convicted in 2009.

In 2015, Italy's Supreme Court definitively acquitted her. The killer was another man, Rudy Guede, convicted years earlier. Knox's "strange" behaviour proved nothing — it was just how a 20-year-old, alone in a foreign country and in shock, behaved.

The mistake of those who judged her was simple: **they compared her to what they would have done, not to her own normal.**

### THE GESTURE SAYS NOTHING. THE CHANGE DOES.

Crossed arms don't mean defensiveness. Looking away doesn't mean lying. Touching your nose means nothing.

This isn't opinion. As you saw in Chapter 1, when 158 supposed signs of lying were reviewed, most showed a weak link or none at all with lying (DePaulo et al., 2003). Some people cross their arms because they're cold. Some avoid eye contact out of shyness, culture or neurodivergence.

What gives someone away isn't the gesture. It's the **change** from that person's normal. That normal is called the **baseline**.

### WHAT THE BASELINE IS

It's how a person behaves when they're relaxed and under no pressure. Watch six channels:

- **Pace of speech** — fast, slow, with pauses?
- **Tone of voice** — deep, high, varied or flat?
- **Eye contact** — constant, intermittent, rare?
- **Hands** — lots of gestures, still, fiddling with objects?
- **Posture** — open, closed, leaning in?
- **Response time** — answers straight away or thinks first?

Only once you know this can you spot deviations. Without a baseline, you're doing what was done to Amanda Knox: comparing the person to yourself.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

The psychologist Paul Ekman named this mistake in 1985: the **Othello error** (Ekman, 1985). In Shakespeare's play, Othello accuses his wife, Desdemona, of betraying him. She cries and denies it — and her tears, which were fear of not being believed, look to him like proof of guilt. Othello kills her. She was innocent.

What Ekman realised is that **an honest person under suspicion looks nervous** — and the nervousness of truth looks just like the nervousness of a lie. If you don't know the baseline, you'll confuse the two.

In other words: without a baseline, you won't just miss lies. You'll **accuse innocent people**. And an unfair accusation destroys a relationship as fast as a betrayal.

### HOW TO ESTABLISH A BASELINE

1. **Start with neutral questions.** How was the weekend, where are they from, what did they do today. Without pressure, the normal shows up.
2. **Observe for at least 3 to 5 minutes.** One minute isn't enough.
3. **Consider the context.** No one is at baseline in a job interview — they're nervous by definition.
4. **Repeat over time.** The baseline of someone you've known for months is worth far more than a stranger's.

### THE DEVIATIONS THAT MATTER

With the baseline in hand, look for **sudden changes tied to a specific topic**:

- **The voice rises half a tone** on one subject.
- **Normally quick answers slow down** on one question.
- **Normally active hands go still** all of a sudden.
- **Normally natural eye contact turns fixed** and rigid.
- **Long explanations appear** where there used to be short answers.

The key word is *topic*. The same 2003 review found that cues become more visible when the person is **highly motivated** and when the lie is about **something wrong they did** (DePaulo et al., 2003). So a deviation that always shows up on the same subject is worth more than ten scattered ones.

> A change is a question, not an answer. It tells you where to look — not what's there.

### THE RULE OF THREE

- **One deviation** — could be coincidence.
- **Two at the same moment** — worth your attention.
- **Three tied to the same topic** — that's a pattern.

That's when it's worth asking more questions — calmly, without accusing. It's Chapter 1's rule applied to the body: a moment says nothing; a pattern says almost everything.

If the Perugia police had used this rule, they'd have seen that Knox's behaviour was strange *across the board* — not on one topic. That's not a sign of lying. It's a sign of shock.

Now that you know what normal is, you need to know where to look first. The place people try hardest to control — and where slips last the shortest — is the face. That's Chapter 3.

Before an important conversation — a deal, an interview, a date — spend the first minutes on neutral topics. It isn't small talk: it's calibration.
[ajuda]
If someone close seems "different", compare them to their normal, not yours. Quieter, fewer messages, less laughter: a shift in a friend's baseline can be the first sign they need help and can't say so.

At Monday's meeting, Sofia is on form: two jokes about the client, an impression of the finance director's accent, everyone laughing. Halfway through, the boss, Marta, says Inês's proposal for the new client "is the best thing this team has produced this month". Sofia picks up her phone. She doesn't speak again until the end of the meeting.

On the way out, she waits for Inês in the corridor. "Congratulations, seriously! Send me the proposal file and I'll polish it so it's even better."

Inês sends it. It's good to have someone on her side.
