# Chapter 18 — Manipulation in Family and Friendships

Los Angeles, June 2021. Britney Spears, one of the most famous singers in the world, speaks in court. For 13 years she has lived under a legal conservatorship controlled largely by her own father: her finances, her career and decisions about her personal life. She describes the situation as abusive and asks the judge to end it.

In November that year, the conservatorship ends.

You don't need to take sides to see the essential point: the control that's hardest to recognise — and to break — is the kind that comes **from family**, wrapped in "it's for your own good".

### WHY THIS MATTERS

In families, manipulation looks like love because it comes from people we love. We grow up with it and call it "normal".

Remember Murray Bowen, in Chapter 13: triangles show up mostly in families, when tension between two people is diverted onto a third — often a child. And psychotherapist Susan Forward named what keeps these patterns alive: **fear, obligation and guilt** — the "fog" that emotional blackmail leaves us in, and that stops us seeing what's going on (Forward, 1997).

In other words: not seeing the pattern in your family isn't a lack of intelligence. It's fog. Seeing the pattern doesn't force you to cut anyone off. It gives you **the choice** of how you relate.

### IN THE FAMILY

- **Guilt as a tool** — "after everything I've done for you". It's reciprocity (Chapter 16) collected forever.
- **Fixed roles** — the "responsible one", the "problem one", the "favourite". Changing roles is seen as betrayal.
- **Conditional love** — affection when you obey, coldness when you don't. It's the intermittent reinforcement from Chapter 14.
- **Secrets and alliances** — one relative tells you things about another to get you on their side. Triangulation (Chapter 13).
- **Boundary invasion** — turning up unannounced, deciding for you, having an opinion on everything.

### IN FRIENDSHIPS

- **The friend who only shows up when they need something.**
- **The friend who competes** — your good news is always topped by theirs.
- **The friend of disguised criticism** — "I'm just being honest".
- **The friend who spreads things** — tells you other people's secrets. They tell yours too.
- **The friend who can't handle your growth** — goes distant when things go well for you.

### THE ENERGY TEST

There's a simple test that works better than any analysis: after you've been with someone, **how do you feel?** Light, motivated, understood? Or tired, small, anxious?

Once is a bad day. Always is a pattern — and you've known since Chapter 1 what a pattern means.

> The right people don't make you feel you have to earn the place beside them.

### HOW TO PROTECT YOURSELF

- **Clear, calm boundaries** — "Thanks for your opinion, but this decision is mine."
- **Don't over-explain.** The more you justify a boundary, the more room you give for negotiating it.
- **Accept that some people won't change.** You can love someone and keep your distance.
- **Choose the dose.** With difficult relatives, it's not just "cut off" or "put up with it". It can be seeing them less often, for less time, in neutral places.

If the energy test is the best detector of bad relationships, there's an equally good detector for the good ones: how real interest behaves when no one's watching. That's Chapter 19.

"Great!" says Sofia. "I was invited to write for them too, last year, but I said no — didn't have time for that sort of thing." She changes the subject to her weekend.

That night, Inês tells Daniel. He asks to read the piece, asks two questions about it and texts her at midnight: "Still thinking about your last line."

The next day, Inês wakes up tired without knowing why. She does the maths: she comes out of almost every lunch with Sofia tired.
