# Chapter 7 — Reading Any Room in 60 Seconds

Swarthmore College, United States, 1951. A student walks into a room for what he's told is a vision test. Seven other people are already seated. They're shown one line and three lines to compare. Which one is the same length? The answer is obvious.

But one by one, the other seven give the **wrong** answer. Out loud. Completely calm.

The student doesn't know the seven are actors working for psychologist Solomon Asch. The experiment was never about vision. The question was: **what does a person do when the whole group says the opposite of what they can see?**

### WHY THIS MATTERS

The answer shocked everyone. Alone, participants almost never got it wrong — less than 1% of the time. In front of the group, they gave the group's wrong answer in about **36%** of trials, and **74%** gave in at least once (Asch, 1951).

But there was an even more important finding. When **a single actor** gave the right answer, almost everyone broke free: only 5% kept following the group.

That proves two things you'll use for the rest of this book. First: **a group changes what people say — and sometimes what they see.** Second: **one ally is enough to break the pressure.** Keep the second one in mind. It'll be decisive in Part IV.

### THE INVISIBLE STRUCTURE

In any meeting, dinner or party there's a structure of power no one says out loud. Four roles show up almost every time:

- **The real Alpha** — not always the loudest. It's the person others look at after someone speaks. Comfortable with silence. When they talk, the room adjusts.
- **The Shadow** — influences without taking centre stage. Says little, but decisions go through them. Watch who the leader consults in private.
- **The Performer** — needs attention. Loud, tells stories, looks for reactions. Often the most insecure person in the room.
- **The Observer** — stays on the edge, says little, sees everything. Might be shy. Or might be the one who worked out first what's going on.

### THE SIGNS IN 60 SECONDS

1. **Where eyes go** when someone makes a joke or a proposal. To whoever has real power.
2. **Who laughs at whose jokes.** Laughter flows up the hierarchy — and says more than it seems. Christopher Oveis and colleagues recorded fraternity students, some high-status, some newcomers. High-status members laughed more **dominantly** — louder and more varied; newcomers more **submissively**. And listeners could guess status from the laugh alone (Oveis et al., 2016).
3. **Who interrupts whom** — and who never gets interrupted.
4. **Where people sit** — head of the table, centre, near the door.
5. **Who speaks first and who speaks last.** Often, the most powerful speaks at the end.
6. **Who moves freely** around the space and who asks permission.

### ALLIANCES AND TENSIONS

- People who **laugh together and mirror each other** are aligned (remember the chameleon effect, Chapter 4).
- People who **avoid each other's eyes** have tension.
- If someone **changes posture when another person walks in**, there's history between them — a deviation from baseline with a topic: that person.

### HOW TO POSITION YOURSELF

- **You don't need to be the Alpha.** You need to know who it is.
- **Talk to the Shadow before the meeting**, not during.
- **Don't feed the Performer** at others' expense.
- **Never underestimate the Observer.**

And remember Asch: if everyone in a room agrees with something that looks wrong to you, **find the one person who doesn't**. One ally is enough.

> The most dangerous person in the room is the one you never thought to look at twice.

You've finished Part I. You can now read masks, baselines, faces, bodies, words, messages and groups. In Part II you'll meet the people who use all of this against you — starting with an uncomfortable question: **why do they choose you?**

:::usa
[protege]
Before proposing something important to a group, identify the Alpha and the Shadow and talk to them first, in private. A proposal backed before the meeting is rarely shot down in it.
[ajuda]
Notice who keeps getting interrupted or ignored. Give them the floor: "Hang on, Anna was saying something." It costs you one sentence — and, as Asch showed, one ally changes everything.
:::

:::treino
At your next meeting or group dinner, don't take part for the first two minutes. Just watch. Identify the Alpha, the Shadow, the Performer and the Observer. Afterwards, check your read against how the decisions went.
:::

End-of-month meeting. Marta makes a joke about the client and the room laughs. Inês, still new, notices where people look when they laugh.

When it's time to choose who leads the new project, Marta says she'll decide tomorrow. On the way out, Inês sees her go into her office with Sofia and close the door.

That night, Rui is on the stairs changing a lightbulb. He hesitates, as if about to say something. "Sofia… does she come to the building often?" Inês says yes, sometimes. He nods and goes back to the bulb.

*What didn't Rui say?*
