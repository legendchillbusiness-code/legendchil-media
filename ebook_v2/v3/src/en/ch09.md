# Chapter 9 — The Dark Triad

Seattle, the 1970s. Ted Bundy is a law student, volunteers on a crisis hotline and is described by people who know him as charming, polite, helpful.

To get close to his victims, he often uses the same trick: an arm in a fake sling and a request for help carrying some books to his car. He doesn't start with force. He uses his victims' wish to **help**.

He was convicted of murdering several women and executed in 1989. Bundy is the extreme — and it's important to say so straight away: the vast majority of people with these traits never commit a crime. They use the same charm at the office, in the family and in love.

### THREE TRAITS THAT TRAVEL TOGETHER

In 2002, psychologists Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams named a pattern that kept turning up in research: three different traits that overlap and often travel together — **narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy**. They called it the **Dark Triad** (Paulhus & Williams, 2002).

The central point of the research is this: these are **traits that come in degrees**. Everyone has a little. What matters isn't whether someone "is" a narcissist — it's *how much*, and what it costs you.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

It might sound like something from the movies. It isn't.

Paul Babiak, Craig Neumann and Robert Hare assessed 203 managers and executives from seven companies, all selected for leadership programmes. About **4%** reached the psychopathy threshold used in research — far above what's estimated for the general population. And the most revealing finding: these managers were rated as **more charismatic and better communicators**, but **worse at actual performance** — management, teamwork, results (Babiak, Neumann & Hare, 2010).

In other words: **charm is exactly what gets them promoted** — and performance is what gives them away. That's the gap from Chapter 1 in its purest form: the Competence Mask winning over people who don't look at results.

### THE NARCISSIST

- **Needs constant admiration.**
- **Reacts badly to criticism** — with anger, coldness or revenge.
- **Sees people as mirrors** — they're worth something as long as they make him feel big.

How you spot him: conversations always come back to him. Your achievements get downplayed or absorbed ("that was thanks to me"). Your problems bore him.

### THE MACHIAVELLIAN

- **Strategic and calculating.**
- **Sees relationships as a game** — who's useful, who isn't.
- **Patient** — can wait months to get what they want.

How you spot them: charming with whoever has power, cold with whoever doesn't. Collects information on everyone. Rarely reveals what they think. Remember the Shadow, in Chapter 7.

### THE FUNCTIONAL PSYCHOPATH

- **Easy, superficial charm** — like Bundy.
- **Lies naturally**, without the stress signals you learned in Part I. That's why the baseline (Chapter 2) and patterns count for more than any in-the-moment signal.
- **Feels no remorse** — only the inconvenience of consequences.

How you spot them: their life story has lots of "enemies", "crazy" exes and burned bridges. Their apologies are words without change.

> The most common sign in all three: the rules they demand of others don't apply to them.

### WHAT TO DO WITH THIS

Don't diagnose anyone. You don't need to know what someone "is" to see **what they're doing to you**. Notice patterns and protect yourself from them.

And notice what Bundy exploited: the wish to help — one of the open doors from Chapter 8. Charm finds the door; the pattern walks through it. In the next chapter you'll see the most common way it's done: **love that arrives too fast**.
