# Chapter 3 — Micro-Signals: Face, Eyes and Mouth

Seattle, the 1990s. Psychologist John Gottman set up an apartment full of cameras that the press nicknamed the "Love Lab". He asked couples to discuss something they disagreed about — and filmed every second.

In one study he followed 124 newlywed couples for six years. From just the **first three minutes** of an argument, he could predict which ones would split (Carrère & Gottman, 1999).

What was he looking for? Not shouting. It was an expression that often lasts less than a second: one corner of the lip rising while the other person speaks. **Contempt.** In the books he wrote later, Gottman describes it as the strongest sign of a relationship heading for the end.

### THE FACE IS WHERE WE CONTROL OURSELVES MOST

The face is the part of the body people try hardest to control — and so it's where slips are most revealing when they happen.

The basic expressions are universal. In the 1960s and 70s, Paul Ekman travelled to Papua New Guinea and showed photos of faces to members of the Fore people, who had almost no contact with the West. They recognised the same emotions Americans did (Ekman & Friesen, 1971). Ekman later added **contempt** to the list (Ekman & Friesen, 1986).

In other words: you don't need to know someone's culture to read their face. But mind the limit — **the face shows an emotion, not the reason for it.** Fear can be guilt. Or fear that you won't believe the truth (remember the Othello error from Chapter 2).

### THE SEVEN EXPRESSIONS

- **Contempt** — only one corner of the lip rises. The only one-sided expression.
- **Disgust** — wrinkled nose, raised upper lip. Rejection of an idea, person or offer.
- **Fear** — brows raised and pulled together, eyes wide, lips stretched.
- **Anger** — brows down and together, hard stare, lips pressed.
- **Surprise** — brows raised, mouth open. It's brief; surprise that lingers is often staged.
- **Sadness** — inner corners of the brows rise, corners of the mouth drop.
- **Joy** — a genuine smile involves the eyes.

Of these, **contempt** matters most. Anger passes. Sadness passes. Contempt means the other person feels *above* you — which is why Gottman saw it as the beginning of the end.

### THE FAKE SMILE

In 1862, the French physician Guillaume Duchenne stimulated volunteers' facial muscles with electricity and noticed something: a real smile doesn't use only the mouth. It activates a muscle around the eyes that's hard to move on purpose. More than a century later, Ekman and colleagues named it the **Duchenne smile** and showed that it appears mainly with genuine enjoyment (Ekman, Davidson & Friesen, 1990).

In practice:

- **Genuine smile** — crow's feet at the eyes, the cheeks lift.
- **Social smile** — only the mouth moves. It appears too fast, stays too long and switches off abruptly.

A social smile isn't a lie — we use it dozens of times a day. But a *mouth-only* smile the moment someone says "I'm so happy for you" is information.

### EYES AND MOUTH

- **Blinking more** — tends to rise under mental load or stress. Compare with the baseline.
- **Staring too hard** — some people know "liars look away" and overcorrect. A rigid, unblinking stare is also a deviation.
- **Eyes closing for longer** — a brief block when they hear something unpleasant.
- **Lips pressed** until they almost disappear — something isn't being said.
- **Covering the mouth** while speaking.

### WHY THIS MATTERS — AND ITS LIMIT

You may have heard of **micro-expressions**: expressions so fast they're barely visible. They exist, but they're rare. When Stephen Porter and Leanne ten Brinke analysed around 700 genuine and faked expressions in high-pressure situations, only **2%** were micro-expressions (Porter & ten Brinke, 2008).

That means two things. First: you won't catch liars by staring at faces waiting for a flash. Second, and more important: the face matters mostly as one piece of a **pattern** — alongside the baseline, the words and the context.

### INCONGRUENCE: THE STRONGEST SIGNAL

What matters most isn't a single expression — it's **incongruence** between face and words:

- They say *"I'm so happy for you"* — and for an instant, contempt.
- They say *"yes"* — while shaking their head.
- They say *"I don't mind"* — with pressed lips.

When words and face disagree, the face is usually closer to the truth. That's what Gottman saw in his couples: the words were polite. The faces weren't.

> The face shows what someone feels. It doesn't tell you why. For that you need context and questions.

People train their faces. Almost nobody trains the rest of the body — which is why, in Chapter 4, we go all the way down to the feet.
