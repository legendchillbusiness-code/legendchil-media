[gancho]
Think of the last person who really fooled you. An ex, a friend, a business partner, a boss.

Now go back to the beginning. The first meeting, the first week. If you're honest, there was something. A sentence that didn't quite fit. A rush you didn't understand. A smile that arrived one second late.

== 1
[caso]
In 2014, Elizabeth Holmes was on the cover of Forbes: the youngest woman to become a self-made billionaire. She promised hundreds of tests from a single drop of blood. Black turtleneck, unblinking stare, a deep voice that, according to former colleagues, wasn't always her own. Investors, former Secretaries of State and one of America's largest pharmacy chains believed her. The technology never worked the way she claimed. In 2022 she was convicted of fraud. The signs were there from the start: huge claims, zero detail, irritation at technical questions. An almost perfect Competence Mask.
[porque]
If you mistake the mask for the face, you hand trust, time and money to a version someone rehearsed — and you only meet the real one once it has cost you. Knowing that masks exist doesn't make you suspicious. It makes you patient: you wait for the gap before you hand over what's yours. And it makes you fairer, because you realise most people wear a mask out of fear, not malice.
[protege]
Before you give someone new access — to your money, your secrets, your home — pick one thing they say they are and wait to see three behaviours that confirm it.
[ajuda]
The Victim Mask often hides real pain. You don't have to be anyone's rescuer, but you can say: "That sounds hard. What can you do next?" You help them step out of the role without leaving them alone.

== 2
[porque]
Without a baseline, you'll see lies where there are only nerves — and accuse people who don't deserve it. The worst reading mistakes don't come from missing signals. They come from seeing signals everywhere. The baseline is what separates someone who reads people from someone who distrusts everyone.
[protege]
Before an important conversation — a deal, an interview, a date — spend the first few minutes on neutral topics. It isn't small talk. It's calibration.
[ajuda]
If someone close to you seems "different", compare them to their normal, not yours. A shift in a friend's baseline — quieter, fewer messages, less laughter — can be the first sign they need help and can't say it.

== 3
[caso]
In the 1990s, psychologist John Gottman filmed hundreds of couples arguing in his Seattle lab, known as the "Love Lab". He found that one of the strongest predictors that a couple would split wasn't shouting or anger. It was contempt: eye-rolling, sarcasm, one corner of the lip rising while the other person spoke.
[porque]
The face is where people try hardest to control themselves — and where the slips last the shortest. If you don't know what to look for, half a second passes and you've missed the most honest information in the whole conversation.
[protege]
When what you're hearing sounds too good, watch the face in the instant right after the sentence. Contempt or a mouth-only smile is a reason to slow down.
[ajuda]
A flash of sadness or fear on a friend who says "I'm fine" is an invitation. Don't contradict them — ask again, calmly: "No, really. How are you?"

== 4
[caso]
Joe Navarro spent 25 years at the FBI in interrogation and counterintelligence. He's the one who popularised the idea that the feet are the most honest part of the body: we learned to run from danger with our legs long before we learned to smile out of politeness.
[porque]
People train their faces to please. Almost nobody trains the rest. If you only watch the face, you're reading the part the person chose to show you.
[protege]
Notice who enters your space too early — a touch on the arm, a hand on your back. Respond with one step back, no words. If they step in again, they were testing you. Now you know.
[ajuda]
In a group, notice who has their feet pointed at the exit and their body pulled in. It's often the person who's uncomfortable and won't say so. One question from you can get them out of there.

== 5
[caso]
In January 1998, Bill Clinton looked into the cameras and said: "I did not have sexual relations with that woman, Miss Lewinsky." Notice "that woman": someone he had worked closely with, referred to like a stranger. Months later, he admitted the relationship. The distancing was in the sentence itself.
[porque]
Words are chosen, but the way someone dodges a question rarely is. Knowing how to listen saves you pointless arguments: instead of arguing about the content, you notice the structure.
[protege]
When the answer to a simple question is complicated, repeat the question — slowly, word for word, without accusing. People telling the truth answer. People managing a story run out of road.
[ajuda]
The open questions in this chapter ("tell me how it went") aren't only for taking stories apart. With someone going through something hard, they're the gentlest way to help them tell a truth that hurts.

== 6
[porque]
Most relationships today start, grow — and break — over text. And texts leave a record. If you know how to read it, you see the change weeks before anyone says it out loud.
[protege]
Don't answer pressure messages ("why did you take so long?") on the spot. Reply at your normal pace. Your pace is your baseline: don't let anyone rewrite it.
[ajuda]
If a friend shows you a chat and asks "am I overreacting?", don't argue about one line. Show them the pattern: who starts, who apologises, who demands.

== 7
[porque]
In every group, some decisions are made before anyone opens their mouth. If you can't see the structure, you spend your energy convincing the wrong person — and never understand why it doesn't work.
[protege]
Before you propose something important to a group, identify the Alpha and the Shadow and talk to them first, in private. A proposal backed before the meeting is rarely shot down in it.
[ajuda]
Notice who keeps getting interrupted or ignored. Give them the floor: "Hang on, Anna was saying something." It costs you one sentence and changes that person's place in the room.

# Chapter X — Why They Choose You

In 1981, two researchers, Betty Grayson and Morris Stein, showed prisoners convicted of violent crimes videos of people walking down a New York street. They asked them who they would pick as a victim.

Within seconds, they kept choosing the same people. Not the smallest or the frailest. The ones who walked with the least assurance: uneven steps, eyes on the ground, movements disconnected from the body.

Years later, another study went further: people who scored higher on psychopathic traits were more accurate at guessing, just from someone's walk, who had been victimised in the past.

Predators read people too. This chapter is about what they read in you.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

This whole book teaches you to read others. But the most important read is yourself. If you don't know where your open doors are, you'll keep recognising the tactics too late — because they were designed to fit those doors.

### WHAT MANIPULATORS LOOK FOR

Not weakness. They look for **usefulness and access**:

- **High empathy** — you feel other people's pain as your own. A manipulator turns it into obligation.
- **Moments of transition** — a breakup, a move, a loss, a lay-off. You're more open and have less of a safety net.
- **Loneliness** — fewer people around you means fewer people to warn you.
- **Need for approval** — trouble saying "no", fear of disappointing.
- **Too much responsibility** — feeling you have to fix everyone's problems.
- **Optimism about people** — "everyone has a good side".

None of these is a flaw. Almost all of them are strengths. That's exactly why they get exploited.

> They don't choose you because you're weak. They choose you because you're useful — and available.

### IT'S NOT YOUR FAULT

Responsibility for manipulation always lies with the manipulator. Full stop. Knowing your open doors isn't accepting blame — it's gaining the ability to close them when you need to.

### HOW TO READ YOURSELF

Answer honestly:

1. What stage of life am I in right now? Stable, or in transition?
2. If something goes wrong, who do I tell?
3. Who do I find hardest to say "no" to?
4. Which compliment disarms me fastest?
5. When did I last feel I had to "earn" someone's affection?

The answers show you where someone would get in.

:::usa
[protege]
In times of transition — a breakup, a move, a loss — slow everything down. No big decisions, no new relationship at full speed. That's when you're easiest to read.
[ajuda]
If someone close to you has just been through a breakup, a move or a loss, get closer during that period. It's when they most need a safety net — and when predators show up most.
:::

:::treino
Pick your two "open doors" from this chapter's list. Next to each one, write the name of a trusted person you'll ask to warn you if someone starts using it.
:::

== 8
[caso]
Ted Bundy was a law student, had volunteered at a crisis hotline and was described as charming, polite and helpful. He wore a fake arm sling to ask for help carrying things to his car, exploiting his victims' willingness to help. He was convicted of murdering several women and executed in 1989. Bundy is the extreme — most people with high Dark Triad traits never commit crimes. They use the same charm at the office, in the family and in love.
[porque]
People with these traits at high levels are a minority, but they cause a disproportionate share of the damage around them. Recognising the pattern early is the difference between one bad date and years of damage.
[protege]
Notice how someone talks about the people who no longer serve them: exes, former partners, old friends. That's how they'll talk about you one day.
[ajuda]
If someone describes a relationship with these patterns, don't say "he's a narcissist". Describe behaviour: "I noticed conversations always come back to him." Labels make people defend him. Facts make them think.

== 9
[caso]
Shimon Hayut called himself Simon Leviev, son of a diamond tycoon. He met women on Tinder and, on the very first date, flew them on a private jet. Then came constant messages, fast declarations, plans for the future. Weeks later came the crisis: "my enemies are after me", blocked cards, an urgent request for money. According to the documentary "The Tinder Swindler" (Netflix, 2022), he took around 10 million dollars from his victims.
[porque]
Love bombing uses the thing we want most — to be seen and loved — as bait. If you don't recognise it, you'll mistake the trap for the best moment of your life and spend the following months trying to get it back.
[protege]
The 90-day rule: no big decisions — money, moving in, cutting someone off — in the first three months. People who care about you will wait. People who want to trap you won't.
[ajuda]
If a friend is "on cloud nine" with someone they met weeks ago, don't attack that person. Ask: "And how does he react when you tell him no?" A question plants a doubt. An attack forces your friend to choose between you and him.

== 10
[porque]
Isolation is the step that makes every other step possible. Alone, nobody tells you "this isn't normal" — and without that outside voice, you accept more and more. Sociologist Evan Stark describes it as a core part of "coercive control", which in England and Wales has been a crime since 2015.
[protege]
Set a fixed meeting with your people — a dinner a month, a call a week — and don't negotiate it with anyone.
[ajuda]
If a friend has drifted away, don't stop inviting them just because they always say no. Keep going, with no guilt or pressure: "No worries. The invite stands." For someone being isolated, knowing the door is still open means everything.

== 11
[caso]
In 1938, playwright Patrick Hamilton premiered "Gas Light": a husband dims the gas lamps in the house and, when his wife notices, insists she's imagining it. In 1944, the film with Ingrid Bergman made the story famous. Today the term means exactly that: someone convincing you that your perception can't be trusted.
[porque]
When you doubt your own memory, you lose the ability to defend yourself — because you're no longer sure there's anything to defend. Gaslighting doesn't just take away your argument. It takes away the ground you stand on.
[protege]
Keep a private, dated log in your phone's notes. Not to prove anything to anyone. To prove it to yourself.
[ajuda]
When someone tells you something and adds "maybe I'm overreacting", validate before you give an opinion: "What you're describing makes sense. I'd feel the same." For someone living with gaslighting, hearing that gives them their memory back.

== 12
[porque]
Triangulation makes you spend energy in the wrong place: competing with a third person instead of looking at whoever created the competition. Psychiatrist Murray Bowen called these "triangles" and showed how they run through entire families. While you're comparing yourself, you don't notice what's being done to you.
[protege]
When you feel new jealousy in an old relationship, ask yourself: "Did this start with me — or with something this person does?"
[ajuda]
In your family or at work, refuse to be the third corner. When someone badmouths another person to pull you onto their side, reply: "That's something you need to talk to her about."

== 13
[caso]
In the 1950s, psychologist B. F. Skinner trained pigeons to peck a key for food. When food came every time, the pigeons pecked as much as they needed. When it came unpredictably, they pecked non-stop — and kept going long after the food stopped. It's the same mechanism as a slot machine. And as certain relationships.
[porque]
Intermittent reinforcement answers the question that creates the most shame: "why don't you just leave?" It isn't weakness. It's conditioning. Understanding that removes the shame — and without shame, people can act.
[protege]
Judge the relationship by its average, not its best day. If the average is bad, the highs are the hook.
[ajuda]
Never tell someone caught in this cycle "just leave". Say: "It makes sense that it's hard. This was built to hold you." Removing the shame is the first step to them getting out.

== 14
[porque]
The most dangerous moment is often not the relationship. It's the return. It comes when you're recovering, with exactly the right words, and one "yes" undoes months of work.
[protege]
Write your reply today for the day this person comes back, and save it on your phone. On that day, you don't think: you read.
[ajuda]
If a friend is being "hoovered" back, remind them of their reasons, not yours: "Remember what you told me in March?" Their memory is worth more than your opinion.

== 15
[caso]
Bernie Madoff had been chairman of NASDAQ. He didn't take just any investor: you had to be introduced, and many waited months to get in. He promised steady returns, year after year, and the most respected names were already inside. Authority, scarcity, social proof and consistency, all at once. In 2008 it was revealed as the largest Ponzi scheme in history, with reported losses of around 65 billion dollars. He was sentenced to 150 years in prison.
[porque]
These triggers work even on smart people — especially on them, because they trust their own judgement. Nobody thinks they can be manipulated. That's exactly what makes you manipulable.
[protege]
Before any "yes" involving money or commitment, ask yourself: "Which trigger am I feeling right now?" Naming it takes away half its power.
[ajuda]
When an older relative tells you about a "once-in-a-lifetime opportunity" or an urgent call from "the bank", help them name the triggers: urgency, authority, scarcity. That's how almost every scam works.

== 16
[porque]
At work, people who can't read others end up doing the work and losing the credit. Competence only counts if it's visible — and if nobody can steal it.
[protege]
At the end of each week, send the decision-maker a three-line update: what you did, what's next, what you need.
[ajuda]
When you see a colleague being erased in a meeting, give them credit in public: "That was Anna's idea." It costs little and shows the whole room you're paying attention.

== 17
[porque]
In families, manipulation looks like love because it comes from people we love. Seeing the pattern doesn't force you to cut anyone off. It gives you a choice about how you relate.
[protege]
Before a difficult family lunch, decide how long you'll stay and what your exit line is. Going with a plan is different from going and hoping.
[ajuda]
If you're the "favourite" or the "responsible one", use that position to protect whoever got the worse role: don't join in the comparisons, and tell them privately that you see what's going on.

== 18
[porque]
Mistaking intensity for interest is one of the most common ways to lose years. Real interest is calm and consistent. If you only recognise fire, you'll keep choosing people who burn you.
[protege]
For one month, judge only by repeated actions. Don't answer words with devotion — answer actions with proportion.
[ajuda]
When a friend asks "do you think he likes me?", don't give an opinion. Ask: "What does he do, repeatedly?" The right question helps more than your answer.

== 19
[porque]
Betrayal hurts most when it catches you by surprise. Seeing the signs early won't prevent it, but it gives you time to protect yourself: accounts, documents, decisions.
[protege]
Every so often, review who has access to what: passwords, shared accounts, documents. Not out of suspicion. Out of hygiene.
[ajuda]
If you suspect a friend is being deceived, don't give them "the news" based on one sign. Share facts, once, carefully, and let them decide: "I saw this. I thought you should know."

== 20
[porque]
Under pressure, nobody thinks clearly. Manipulators count on it: that you'll search for the perfect reply and, while you search, give in. A prepared line keeps you standing. The broken-record technique was described by psychologist Manuel J. Smith in the 1970s and is still one of the most effective.
[protege]
Save the lines from this chapter's practice in a phone note called "Lines". Reread it before difficult conversations.
[ajuda]
Rehearse these lines with a friend who's going through something hard. Saying them out loud to someone you trust makes it easier to say them to the person who needs to hear them.

== 21
[porque]
A boundary you don't enforce teaches the other person exactly how far they can go. Without boundaries, reading people is useless: you see everything — and accept everything.
[protege]
Start with small boundaries. Keeping a small one ("if you're half an hour late, I'll leave") trains you to keep the big ones.
[ajuda]
When someone close sets a boundary — even with you — respect it the first time and say: "Understood." You're showing them what a healthy reaction looks like. Many people have never seen one.

== 22
[porque]
Leaving is when the cycle fights hardest to keep you. People who leave without a plan often go back — not out of weakness, but because the cycle was designed for it. It often takes several attempts to leave for good. Every attempt counts.
[protege]
Don't announce anything until the essentials are ready (see "Before you leave"). The plan is your protection.
[ajuda]
If someone close went back to the relationship, never say "I told you so". Say: "I'm here whenever you need me." People who go back and feel ashamed stop talking. People who feel the door is open leave again.

# Chapter X — How to Help Someone Who's Trapped

Watching a friend in a relationship that's destroying them is one of the hardest things to witness. The instinct is to tell them the truth, hard: "He's toxic. Get out."

It almost always backfires. Your friend defends the person, feels judged and pulls away — from you. Which is exactly what the manipulator wants.

### WHY THIS MATTERS

People who are trapped rarely leave on their own. They leave because someone on the outside never stopped being there. Everything else in this book is about protecting yourself. This chapter is about protecting the people you love.

### WHAT DOESN'T WORK

- **Attacking the person** — it forces your friend to defend them.
- **Ultimatums** — "him or me" pushes them deeper into isolation.
- **Labels** — "narcissist", "psychopath" sound like exaggeration and shut the conversation down.
- **Rushing** — the decision to leave has to be theirs, in their time.

### WHAT WORKS

1. **Stay.** Your presence is exactly what isolation is trying to destroy. Keep in touch, even if it's one message a week.
2. **Ask, don't tell.** "How do you feel after you've been together?" "What happens when you tell him no?" Questions let them reach conclusions on their own — and their own conclusions are the only ones that stick.
3. **Talk about behaviour, not character.** "I've noticed you don't come to dinners any more" instead of "he's cutting you off from us".
4. **Validate what they feel.** "It makes sense that you're confused." People living with gaslighting need someone to confirm reality.
5. **Remind them who they are.** Talk about what they used to love, the people they were close to, how they were before. The manipulator rewrote their identity. You keep the original.
6. **Help with the practical when they ask.** A place to stay, help moving, company for a difficult conversation.

> You don't have to save them. You have to be the place they can come back to.

### WHEN THERE'S DANGER

If there's violence, threats or real fear, everything changes: safety comes first. Domestic-abuse and victim-support services also help people who want to support someone else. In immediate danger, call your local emergency number (112 in Europe, 911 in the US).

### AND YOU

Helping someone who's trapped is exhausting. You're allowed boundaries here too: you can be available without being available all the time. Look after your energy so you can keep going.

Think of someone who once seemed trapped in a relationship — romantic, a friendship or at work. Write one open question you could ask them, with no judgement. If it still makes sense, ask it this week.
:::
